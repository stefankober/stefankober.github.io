      • Participation

      5 minStefan Kober

      We began by asking what in our lives can properly be called our doing. Choice seemed an obvious place to look.

      Choice is real. We can stop, consider possibilities, select one, and act on it. But choice did not mark the boundary we were looking for. Something can be our doing without being preceded by a separate choice at every step.

      A learned activity may unfold while we monitor what happens. We may intervene only when something calls for correction. Preparation done long before may become effective in the moment. An earlier course may be maintained against resistance or abandoned when it no longer holds.

      A chess move may be preceded by deliberation, while a movement in sport emerges from training and the situation. We can also initiate a calculation deliberately even though its result is not ours to choose.

      These are not merely stronger and weaker versions of one process. They are different ways in which something can be our doing.

      Choice is one of them. It is not the whole of participation.

      Sometimes our reasons are easy to state.

      You choose the screwdriver because you want to tighten the screw. The screwdriver is not simply better than the hammer or the knife. It is better for what you are trying to do.

      Sometimes a reason becomes visible when we intervene.

      You stop because something looks dangerous. You change course because another possibility has become available. But a reason need not accompany every part of an activity separately.

      Someone may be dancing, skating, playing an instrument, or riding a motorcycle.

      Why that exact movement? There may be no useful answer.

      Why continue the activity? There may be one. A reason can belong to the larger course without explaining each movement within it.

      Sometimes the question arises only afterwards.

      Why did I say that?

      Why did I stop?

      Why did I stay?

      Why did I keep returning to this?

      An answer may form. Maybe I was afraid, wanted approval, or cared more than I had realized. Maybe the old course no longer convinced me. Such answers are convictions too. They can stabilize, change, or dissolve.

      A later explanation of our own action is not automatically the true motive. It is another attempt to understand our participation.

      Patterns of doing can nevertheless tell us something about orientation.

      We return to some questions, protect some things, prepare for some possibilities, and maintain some courses while abandoning others.

      No single action need reveal very much. Across time, however, what we repeatedly do can provide evidence about what matters, what we expect, and what has become sufficiently stable to guide us.

      None of this begins from an empty situation. We enter circumstances already underway: a language we speak, practices we learned, institutions and relationships already there, events that happened before we arrived, and convictions and distinctions we did not originate. Orientation precedes any particular act of reflection or choice. Yet what we do within it can alter what happens next.

      We can ask a question that another person later finds difficult to answer. We can introduce a possibility they had not considered. We can teach a procedure, preserve a comparison, challenge a claim, or build something that remains available after the action itself is over. Earlier action can become part of the conditions under which later action and conviction occur.

      The result is not under the original actor's command.

      A question may be ignored, a possibility fail to convince, or a method be learned and later rejected. A promise can change another person's expectations in ways we did not foresee.

      What we do can matter without determining what follows.

      The cases examined here do not reveal one obvious mechanism common to everything we call our doing.

      Explicit choice is sometimes present and sometimes absent, and control may be extensive or limited. Preparation can occur long before action, while fluent activity may outrun conscious deliberation. Consequences can also follow from what we do without having been separately intended.

      Comprehensive control is not required for something to count as our doing. Nor does our doing reduce to isolated moments of explicit choice.

      Participation names this broader involvement.

      It does not explain the metaphysics of agency. It marks a difference that the examples repeatedly make visible: between something merely happening around us and our having a part in what happens.

      That is enough for Conviction Formation Theory as a preliminary account of how finite agents can continue to inquire and act without direct control of conviction or final settlement.

      We do not choose directly what will convince us, whether a deliberately posed question will become genuinely puzzling, or which possibility will eventually acquire force.

      We can nevertheless ask, search, prepare, compare, practise, challenge, defend, and enter procedures whose results are not ours to determine.

      What we do can alter the conditions under which conviction forms. Those altered conditions can in turn become part of someone else's orientation.

      We do not stand outside conviction formation and direct it. But neither are we absent from it.
