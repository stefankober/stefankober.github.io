      • Revoking A Choice

      3 minStefan Kober

      Suppose you decided to go running tomorrow morning.

      Tomorrow comes.

      You wake up and think:

      No. I am not going.

      You may even say it aloud.

      The earlier choice has been revoked.

      This seems straightforward.

      You chose one thing yesterday.

      Today you choose otherwise.

      The later choice replaces the earlier one.

      But not every change of heart works that way.

      Suppose you decided never to answer work messages after eight in the evening.

      Then one evening a colleague writes: "The server is down."

      You reply. Perhaps you do not think you have abandoned the rule at all.

      The situation simply does not seem to be one to which it applies.

      The earlier choice remains.

      What changed is how the present situation is understood.

      Sometimes even that does not happen.

      You made an investment plan.

      You still think the plan is good.

      You still think you should follow it.

      Then the market falls sharply.

      You watch the losses grow.

      And you sell.

      You may still be convinced, even while doing so, that selling is contrary to the strategy you chose.

      The earlier choice has not been revoked.

      The rule has not been reinterpreted.

      Something else has become strong enough to override it in action.

      Or suppose you decided to finish a difficult task today.

      You work for an hour.

      Then you stop.

      Not because you have abandoned the plan.

      Not because you now think it was a bad plan.

      You tell yourself:

      Not now. I will continue later.

      The choice remains in place, but its enactment is suspended.

      Whether you return to it is another matter.

      And sometimes there is no identifiable moment of reversal at all.

      You once decided to practise an instrument every evening.

      For a while you do.

      Then one evening you do not.

      A few days later you miss another.

      Eventually weeks pass.

      Perhaps you still vaguely think of yourself as someone who practises every evening.

      But the old choice has lost its force.

      No second decision replaced it.

      You never clearly said: I no longer intend to do this.

      You simply stopped.

      An earlier choice can lose its influence in different ways.

      It can be explicitly revoked.

      Its application can be reconsidered.

      It can remain convincing and still be overridden in action.

      It can be suspended.

      Or it can gradually cease to shape what happens, without any clear later choice taking its place.

      This makes it difficult to understand a life as a sequence of choices governing what follows.

      Choices matter.

      They can redirect what happens.

      They can leave rules, habits, promises, and altered circumstances behind.

      These can help an earlier choice survive later pressure.

      We can make some actions easier, others harder, create reminders, establish routines, or involve other people.

      Changes in circumstances can have the opposite effect. A routine that seemed stable at home may disappear during a journey, a move, or another interruption of ordinary life.

      Any choice can be revised, resisted, forgotten, or simply lose its force.

      An earlier choice does not govern what follows merely because it was once ours.

      And also here, there need not be a choice at all.
