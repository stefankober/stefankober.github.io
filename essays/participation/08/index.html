      • Doing Across Time

      4 minStefan Kober

      Suppose you decide to build a table.

      You draw a rough plan, buy wood, measure twice, and cut once. You still make a mistake and replace one piece. Then you leave the work unfinished for a week and return to it later. Eventually the table stands in front of you.

      You built it. That description seems natural.

      But there was no single moment in which building the table happened.

      There were many actions, some deliberate, some habitual, some corrected halfway through, and some probably forgotten by the time the work was done.

      The original choice to build a table mattered. But it did not govern every movement that followed.

      And the table was not built by the choice alone.

      The same is true when learning a piece of music, training for a race, restoring a neglected garden, or writing a book. These activities can take weeks, months, or years. They contain pauses, changed plans, mistakes, new ideas, and periods in which little happens at all.

      The original reason for beginning may remain present throughout, change over time, or disappear almost completely while the activity continues in another form.

      Something that began as a side effect can even become the main reason for continuing.

      Still, afterwards we say:

      "I learned this."

      "I restored that."

      "I wrote it."

      The doing extends across time even though no single choice extends across time in the same way.

      What we call one doing may contain many smaller doings, choices, revisions, and periods in which no choice is present at all.

      Consider writing a book.

      Maybe you begin with a clear idea of what it should become. Later that idea no longer convinces you. You remove a chapter, and another may appear unexpectedly. At some point you might abandon the project for months because nothing seems to work. When you return, you may no longer remember some of the reasons behind earlier decisions.

      Yet those decisions remain in the text.

      Some sentences were carefully constructed. Others appeared quickly and survived every later revision. Someone else may have suggested a change that became indispensable.

      The finished book contains all of this.

      It would be strange to conclude that you did not write it because no single plan or choice remained unchanged from beginning to end.

      What makes the book your doing is not that every part of its history was chosen in advance.

      You repeatedly returned to it, altered it, abandoned parts, added others, and brought it closer to what it eventually became.

      This seems to be true beyond individual projects.

      A household can maintain a home over many years.

      No single decision of one person does that. People clean and repair things, buy and replace them, forget some problems and notice others, organize, neglect, and begin again. Different people contribute different things at different times.

      Still, it can make sense to say that they maintained the home together.

      A company may build a product that no single person designed or made. Towns preserve infrastructure and practices across generations, and scientific fields can develop methods through the work of people who never met one another.

      Here the language of doing begins to widen.

      The participants change. The choices change. Some contributions are deliberate and visible. Others become routines, institutions, tools, or inherited practices. What one person leaves behind becomes part of the situation in which another acts.

      The larger the doing becomes, the harder it is to identify one chooser behind it. Yet the doing does not disappear. It becomes distributed across actions, time, and people.

      Earlier choices can shape later conditions. Later actions can revise what earlier participants began. Some contributions remain visible. Others disappear into the structure of what has been built.

      We may choose some parts. Others may arise from habit, training, circumstances, or what others have already done. Yet they can all become part of what we did.

      This is one reason participation is a better word than control. We do not need to have chosen the whole course of something in order to have had a part in bringing it about. And what we bring about can become part of the conditions under which others act after us.
