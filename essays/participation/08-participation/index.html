      • Participation

      5 minStefan Kober

      We began by asking what in our lives can properly be called our doing.

      Choice seemed an obvious place to look.

      Choice is real.

      We can stop.

      Consider possibilities.

      Select one.

      Act on it.

      But choice did not mark the boundary we were looking for.

      Instead, the examples show several ways in which something can be our doing.

      Choice is one way in which we participate.

      It is not the only one.

      Sometimes we choose and then act.

      Sometimes a learned activity unfolds while we monitor what happens.

      Sometimes we intervene only when something calls for correction.

      Sometimes preparation done long before becomes available in the moment.

      Sometimes an earlier course is maintained against resistance.

      Sometimes it is abandoned.

      A chess move may be preceded by deliberation.

      A movement in sport may emerge from training and the situation.

      A promise may be maintained simply because we remember that we made it.

      A habit may continue long after the choice that helped establish it has disappeared from memory.

      These do not all look like stronger and weaker versions of one process.

      In each case we participate.

      But not in the same way.

      There is another difference.

      Sometimes we can say why we did something.

      You choose the screwdriver because you want to tighten the screw.

      The criterion belongs to the choice.

      The screwdriver is not simply better than the hammer or the knife.

      It is better for what you are trying to do.

      Sometimes the why appears when we intervene.

      You stop because something looks dangerous.

      You change course because another possibility has become visible.

      But not every part of our doing seems to have such a why.

      A fluent movement may lead into the next without a separate reason for that movement.

      You may be dancing, skating, playing an instrument, or riding a motorcycle.

      Why that exact movement?

      There may be no answer.

      Why are you doing the activity at all?

      There may be one again.

      The why can belong to the whole without belonging to every part.

      Sometimes the why becomes visible only afterwards.

      Why did I say that?

      Why did I stop?

      Why did I stay?

      Why did I keep returning to this?

      An answer may form.

      Perhaps I was afraid.

      Perhaps I wanted approval.

      Perhaps I cared more than I knew.

      Perhaps I simply no longer found the old course convincing.

      These answers are convictions too.

      They can themselves change.

      A later explanation is not automatically the true motive.

      It is another attempt to understand our own participation.

      Sometimes that attempt becomes convincing.

      Sometimes it does not.

      Where motives are present, they connect our doing to our orientation.

      To what matters.

      To what we protect.

      To what we prepare for.

      To what we remain with.

      To what we oppose.

      These things need not be present as explicit thoughts.

      Often they become visible only across many situations.

      We return to certain questions.

      Protect some things.

      Let others go.

      Over time, doing leaves traces of orientation.

      It is easy to agree on trees.

      We can point.

      Walk over.

      Touch the bark.

      Measure the trunk.

      If one person says there is a tree and another says there is none, there are usually things we can do together that settle conviction quickly.

      Other things are harder.

      What matters?

      What is worth doing?

      What should be preserved?

      When should we continue?

      When should we stop?

      What kind of life is worth living?

      Here we do not have the tree in front of us in the same way.

      Convictions form through longer histories.

      Through experience.

      Questions.

      Stories.

      Other people.

      Practice.

      Affect.

      Formal reasoning where it applies.

      Circumstances we may or may not have participated in creating.

      And through what we ourselves do.

      It is not as easy to agree.

      But we still have to live.

      We enter situations already underway.

      The language we speak.

      The practices we learned.

      The institutions we encounter.

      The convictions we inherited.

      The events that happened before we arrived.

      And still we do things.

      We notice.

      We respond.

      We begin.

      We stop.

      We prepare.

      We practise.

      We continue what others began.

      We alter things that were already there.

      Sometimes deliberately.

      Sometimes almost without noticing.

      And often without seeing everything that follows.

      Our doing does not require that we created the situation in which it occurs.

      Nor does it require that we control everything that comes from it.

      The different forms of doing things do not seem to reveal one common mechanism.

      Yet in each of them there seems to be a difference between something merely happening around us and our having a part in what happens.

      What exactly makes that difference remains unclear.

      Apparently we can recognize participation more easily than we can explain it.

      What we do can also outlast the moment in which we do it.

      A tool remains.

      A habit is learned.

      A promise changes what another person expects.

      A question is passed on.

      A method is taught.

      A building stands.

      A story is told again.

      What one person does becomes part of the conditions under which another acts.

      Convictions move through such conditions as well.

      We inherit questions, practices, stories, distinctions, and ways of seeing.

      They help form what convinces us.

      We question some of them.

      Reinforce others.

      Change them.

      Forget them.

      Pass them on.

      We do not stand outside conviction formation and direct it.

      But neither are we absent from it.

      We participate.

      What happens to us shapes the situation in which we act.

      What we do becomes part of what happens next.

      And what happens next may become part of the world in which someone else begins.
