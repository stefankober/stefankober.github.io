      • The Reach Of Choice

      4 minStefan Kober

      Suppose you set an alarm before going to sleep.

      You choose the time. Maybe you have an early appointment or want to go running.

      Then you sleep. Hours later the alarm rings.

      The choice happened yesterday. It changed what happens now, but the choice itself is no longer happening.

      You may not even remember immediately why you set the alarm.

      And when it rings, another situation has already formed.

      You are tired. The bed is warm. Getting up may no longer seem like a particularly good idea.

      The earlier choice still matters. But it does not simply govern the later moment.

      Sometimes we choose earlier precisely because we expect the later situation to change what seems convincing.

      You are angry. You want to send a message.

      But you know that anger changes what appears reasonable.

      So you decide: Not tonight.

      You put the phone away.

      Maybe you even write the message and leave it unsent.

      The choice changes the conditions under which you will act later.

      It creates distance. It gives other convictions time to return. In the morning, the message may still seem right. Or embarrassing. Or unnecessary.

      The same structure can extend much further.

      An investor decides in calm circumstances how to respond to a large fall in the market.

      The rule is simple: Do not sell merely because prices fall.

      Years later prices collapse. Losses become visible, other investors panic, and news stories predict disaster. The conviction that selling immediately is the only sensible thing to do may become very strong.

      The earlier strategy still exists. Maybe even in writing. But the earlier choice has not prevented the later conviction from forming. The person who made the rule and the person facing the crash are the same person. Yet they do not stand in the same situation.

      What convinced the person in one set of circumstances does not necessarily convince them in another.

      So what remains after a choice has been made?

      Sometimes what remains is a memory, a rule, an alarm, a promise, a habit, a note left for oneself, or other people who remember what we said we would do.

      The choice can leave many things behind. But these things are not the choice itself.

      They participate in what happens later.

      The earlier choice need also not remain present in memory.

      You may get up when the alarm rings without remembering why you chose that particular time. If the alarm still rings because you once set it, the earlier choice remains plainly involved in what happens now.

      Other cases are different.

      You may have stopped sending angry messages years ago and now simply wait before replying. An earlier decision may have helped form that habit.

      But today's waiting is not simply the earlier choice continuing to act. The connection is historical rather than immediate.

      The investment strategy is looser still.

      An earlier choice may remain available as a rule or reminder. But when the market falls, the later action arises in a new situation. You may be forced to choose again.

      What remains of a choice differs from case to case.

      It may be preserved in something we deliberately set in motion, survive through habits it helped form, or remain only as one consideration among others.

      The history matters without becoming a permanent command.

      We can create reminders, change the environment, establish rules, build habits, create obligations, and make some actions easier or harder.

      A choice occurs at a particular time.

      Choice can reach into the future.

      But it does not control it.

      We may continue what we once chose, revise it, or later act without any identifiable choice at all.

      Yet all of these can still be our doing.
