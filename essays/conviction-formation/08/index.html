      • 8. Destabilization and Change

      4 minStefan Kober

      Convictions do not only form and stabilize.

      They weaken, fracture, become qualified or reorganized, and sometimes disappear.

      The processes are often easiest to see when something that once felt obvious stops doing so.

      Contradiction and Tension

      Suppose Peter is accused of a crime.

      A witness identifies him at the scene.

      But Peter's friend insists that Peter was with him somewhere else at the relevant time.

      If both claims initially convince you, they cannot remain comfortably side by side once the conflict becomes salient.

      The witness may have been mistaken, or the friend may be lying. Perhaps the timeline was misunderstood, Peter left and returned, or one of the reports was described inaccurately.

      The contradiction does not determine which conviction should change.

      It creates pressure.

      People respond to such pressure in different ways.

      They may revise or qualify one conviction, reinterpret the situation, compartmentalize the conflict, avoid it, or abandon one of the convictions.

      Sometimes they tolerate inconsistency for a long time until circumstances force it into attention.

      A contradiction that is never noticed need not destabilize anything.

      A contradiction that becomes salient may reorganize an entire field.

      Formal systems make some contradictions difficult to ignore because the conflicting consequences can be explicitly reconstructed. Everyday life is often less tidy.

      Questions

      A question can also destabilize without directly asserting the opposite of a conviction.

      Suppose you have become convinced that a colleague ignored you deliberately.

      Then someone asks: "What else could explain it?"

      All of a sudden, alternatives may reopen.

      The colleague may not have seen you, may have been distracted, or perhaps was responding to something else.

      A question can turn what had been treated as settled back into something examinable, make an assumption visible, redirect attention, or create space around a conclusion.

      This does not mean questioning always weakens conviction.

      A challenge may strengthen it.

      Unanswered questions may disappear.

      Good questions may expose an overlooked difficulty, while a bad one merely distracts.

      The role of questions is large enough to require separate treatment.

      For now, it is enough that questions can reopen parts of orientation that had become relatively closed.

      Failed Practice

      Some convictions weaken in use.

      Suppose you are convinced that a particular shortcut saves time.

      You take it repeatedly.

      Again and again, traffic makes the route slower.

      At some point the conviction may become difficult to sustain.

      A software diagnostic procedure may work reliably for years and then begin identifying the wrong component after an architectural change.

      The same pattern can occur when a theory repeatedly predicts events that fail to happen, or when an interpretation of someone's behavior keeps leading to surprise.

      Here the destabilizing force comes from breakdown.

      Something relied upon stops working as expected.

      This does not determine exactly what should be revised. But it can make continued reliance harder to sustain.

      Repeated breakdown can become a source of destabilization.

      Loss of Reinforcement

      Imagine someone leaving a close-knit religious community.

      For years, particular convictions were renewed through conversation, ritual, shared texts, friendships, calendars, and expectations.

      After leaving, the person may initially remain just as convinced.

      But some of the conditions that repeatedly returned those convictions to attention are gone.

      Months or years later, things that once seemed obvious may feel more distant.

      The same can occur when leaving a profession, political movement, social group, relationship, or institution.

      Reinforcement can be one condition of stability.

      Remove the condition and stability may change.

      Sometimes the opposite happens. Leaving a group strengthens a conviction precisely because opposition makes it more salient.

      Transformation and Dissolution

      Not every destabilized conviction disappears.

      Suppose someone believes: "My father was a bad person."

      Later they learn more about his circumstances.

      The conviction may weaken.

      Or narrow: "He treated us badly during those years."

      It may become conditional: "I still think what he did was wrong, but I no longer think it defines him."

      It can transform: "I used to understand his behavior as cruelty. I now understand much of it as fear."

      Or dissolve: "I simply don't see him that way anymore." Another conviction may replace it.

      These changes are not equivalent.

      Weakening concerns force, while narrowing restricts scope. Conditionalization adds conditions that were previously absent. A conviction can also be transformed, dissolved, or replaced by another.

      The boundaries are not always clear.
