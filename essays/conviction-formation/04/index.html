      • 4. Conviction Forms Against a Background

      4 minStefan Kober

      Imagine receiving a short message from a close friend: "Fine."

      What does it mean?

      There is no answer in the word alone.

      If you have just proposed meeting at seven, it may mean agreement.

      If you have been arguing for an hour, it may sound irritated.

      If your friend habitually writes in short messages, nothing may seem unusual.

      If they normally write warmly and expansively, the same word may become alarming.

      A tiny piece of information enters an already structured situation.

      The situation helps determine what the message can become evidence for.

      Conviction forms against such a background. When something new occurs, much is already in place.

      We have previous convictions, expectations, language, concepts, memories, skills, habits, and relationships. We have distinctions, ways of interpreting situations, social practices, physical circumstances, questions already occupying us, and practical commitments.

      New information enters this background and may confirm, disturb, extend, or reorganize parts of it.

      This presently operative background through which a person finds their bearings can be called orientation.

      It may include convictions, but it is broader than conviction. An unresolved question, a practical skill, an unformulated expectation, a perceived possibility, or a commitment such as having promised to meet someone tomorrow can all be part of someone's orientation. So can memories and relationships that make some interpretations immediately plausible and others remote.

      Orientation is an organizing term for these already operative conditions.

      This matters because new convictions depend partly on what is already there.

      Consider a toddler and an astronomer looking at the night sky.

      The same visible points of light can participate in very different orientations.

      The astronomer may see a planet, recognize a constellation, notice atmospheric distortion, or infer something from apparent movement.

      The toddler does not merely possess fewer propositions about the same scene. Different distinctions are available, other questions can arise, and different things may become salient.

      Or consider a sentence in an unfamiliar language.

      For someone who cannot read it, the marks may be recognizable as writing without carrying much further structure.

      For a fluent reader, meaning can appear almost automatically.

      Training has changed what the same marks can do.

      New information does not arrive into an empty space. It is encountered through the active elements of an existing orientation.

      Stories

      One especially important way human beings organize situations is through stories.

      Suppose you see a colleague leave a meeting abruptly.

      You might connect the event with what happened earlier.

      Someone may have insulted her.

      She may have received an urgent message.

      Perhaps she was angry about a decision.

      Or she simply had another appointment.

      The event acquires different meanings depending on the surrounding story.

      Stories can organize who is involved, what happened, how events connect, what preceded them, what people intended, what might happen next, and what matters about all of it.

      This is not restricted to polished narratives.

      Much everyday understanding has this form.

      Someone says:

      "He has been distant since Monday."

      "We were getting along until that argument."

      "She promised to call, but then this happened."

      The events are being connected into a provisional whole that makes further expectation and action possible.

      Stories can participate strongly in conviction formation. If you become convinced that a colleague deliberately humiliated you, later events may be interpreted against that background. A neutral remark can acquire significance. An apology may be treated as insincere. A forgotten meeting may become further evidence.

      We often organize particular situations this way. A disagreement with a friend becomes part of the story of that relationship. A difficult year becomes part of the story of a life. Events at work become part of a story about an institution, a profession, or how things are done there. These stories often connect with broader ones about groups, places, societies, or a life as a whole.

      Other parts of orientation need not be organized primarily as stories. Navigating a room may rely mainly on spatial orientation. Performing a familiar task can be procedural. A mathematical proof may be represented symbolically or diagrammatically. An affective response may arise before any story about it becomes available.

      Orientation is the broader term. Stories are one important way in which parts of it can be organized and connected.
