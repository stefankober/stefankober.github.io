      • 5. Recurring Conditions of Conviction Formation

      6 minStefan Kober

      We can now look more closely at some conditions under which convictions form and acquire force.

      What follows is not an exhaustive list.

      Some are ordinary observations. Some are plausible interpretations of those observations. Their interaction is likely to vary substantially between persons, domains, and situations.

      The purpose is to identify recurring patterns worth investigating.

      Attention and Salience

      Imagine walking through a city with an architect.

      You notice shops, people, traffic, perhaps whether the street feels pleasant.

      The architect notices window proportions, materials, load-bearing structures, the transition between old and new buildings.

      Walk the same street with a security specialist and different things may stand out again.

      Entrances, blind corners, lighting, and crowd movement for example.

      Nothing requires the three people to disagree.

      They may simply be attending to different things.

      Reasoning does not usually begin from a neutral inventory of everything present.

      Something first becomes relevant enough to enter the process.

      A remark that previously passed unnoticed becomes an insult.

      Cracks in a wall may suddenly matter once you know what structural movement looks like.

      A teacher can point out a musical detail that you had never noticed before.

      And after learning what a symptom can indicate, it may become alarming.

      Attention can sometimes be directed deliberately.

      Look here. Compare these two. Listen to the bass line. Check what happens immediately before the error.

      But salience can also be produced by events themselves.

      A loud noise interrupts. Pain captures attention. A frightening possibility becomes difficult not to consider.

      Texts, diagrams, interfaces, environments, and other people can all reorganize what stands out.

      Differences in conviction begin before explicit disagreement.

      One person sees a problem. Another does not yet see anything there to disagree about.

      What becomes salient can alter what becomes available for further conviction formation.

      Perception and Affect

      Some convictions acquire force without first passing through an explicit argument.

      You look outside and see that it is raining. You hear a crash and become convinced that something fell.

      In other cases, affect participates in what becomes convincing. A remark strikes you as cruel. Someone you trusted suddenly seems threatening. A piece of music strikes you as beautiful.

      These are distinguishable cases. Perception, affect, evaluation, and interpretation may contribute in different ways. What they share is only that explicit reasoning need not come first.

      Pain, fear, disgust, attraction, empathy, and anger can change what becomes salient and how a situation is interpreted before any explicit argument is made. Anger, for example, can temporarily make hostile interpretations seem compelling and alternatives remote. When it subsides, some of that convincing force may disappear with it.

      Experience, Repetition, and Practice

      Convictions can acquire stability through repeated experience.

      A friend repeatedly keeps promises.

      The same instrument gives the expected reading again and again.

      A familiar procedure continues to produce the expected result.

      Trust can grow without any single decisive event.

      But repetition does not strengthen conviction by itself.

      Exposure can produce boredom or resistance, something initially striking can disappear into the background, and repeated failure can weaken confidence instead.

      What matters is what happens through repeated encounters within a particular orientation. Repeated success can stabilize a conviction. Repeated failure can weaken one. Neither result follows from repetition alone.

      Experience and practice can also change the orientation against which later convictions form.

      Consider learning to drive.

      At first, many things require deliberate attention.

      Mirrors.

      Clutch.

      Gear.

      Speed.

      Lane position.

      Other vehicles.

      With practice, distinctions that once demanded effort become readily available, and some responses become almost automatic.

      Something similar happens in more specialized practices.

      An experienced radiologist may notice distinctions in an image that a beginner misses. Similar changes occur in other learned practices: a musician begins to recognize relations that once passed unnoticed, or a mathematician sees structures that previously required laborious reconstruction.

      Practice has not merely added information. It has changed what can be noticed, recognized, or handled readily.

      Whatever the underlying cognitive explanation, experience and learned practices can alter both existing convictions and the conditions under which later conviction forms.

      Interaction and Social Reinforcement

      Convictions also form between people.

      You tell someone what happened.

      They believe you.

      Or they look doubtful.

      Someone points out an alternative you had not considered.

      A group may laugh at a remark.

      The teacher corrects your answer.

      A colleague says, "That is exactly what I saw."

      Or a friend asks, "Are you sure?"

      Such responses can reinforce, destabilize, redirect, or leave conviction untouched.

      People encounter not only explicit instruction, but approval, correction, imitation, surprise, disapproval, and expectations about what "people like us" do.

      Over time, some of these patterns can become deeply familiar.

      Similar processes continue in adult life.

      Professions and institutions preserve methods and procedures. Religious, scientific, familial, and political communities can carry texts, practices, expectations, categories, and standards across long periods.

      Convictions can acquire part of their stability through participation in durable social structures.

      And when those structures change, conviction may change with them.

      Integration

      Some convictions are hard to disturb because many parts of a person's orientation support them at once.

      Suppose someone has been convinced for decades that close friendship is important.

      That conviction may connect with memories, habits, plans, identity, relationships, moral judgments, and expectations about a worthwhile life.

      Challenging it is not like replacing one isolated item in a list.

      Many things may have to move.

      A political or religious conviction may become connected with relationships, identity, memories, practices, and ways of interpreting events. Convictions about oneself can similarly affect which ambitions seem possible and which failures seem intelligible.

      This suggests a strong hypothesis:

      Convictions integrated with many active parts of orientation may often resist local disturbance more strongly than relatively isolated convictions.

      But the hypothesis should not be mistaken for a principle of justification.

      Integration can support false convictions.

      A conspiracy theory can integrate observations, identity, community, and distrust into a highly resilient structure.

      Removing one piece may accomplish little because the rest continues to hold.

      This is a hypothesis about persistence, not about merit. A conviction does not become better justified merely because many parts of a person's orientation support it.

      That distinction matters because the same conditions can play different roles. Reasons, evidence, methods, and criteria may give us grounds for judging one conviction better supported than another. They may also participate in changing what someone is actually convinced of. Since conviction is not under direct voluntary control, these two questions cannot simply be collapsed: what should convince us, and what in fact becomes convincing, are related but distinct.

      Here we are concerned with the second question. We can investigate why an integrated conviction survives local challenge without treating that survival as evidence in its favor.

      Integration may explain persistence. It does not vindicate what persists.
