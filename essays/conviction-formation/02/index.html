      • 2. What Is a Conviction?

      3 minStefan Kober

      Suppose someone tells you: "The meeting starts at ten."

      You may repeat the sentence without believing it. You may have been asked to pass the message along.

      You may act on it without being convinced. Maybe your manager told you to arrive at ten and you obey, although you privately suspect the meeting actually starts at eleven.

      You may agree for strategic reasons. An argument about the starting time can have become tiresome and you say, "Fine, you're right," merely to end it.

      You may also genuinely expect the meeting to begin at ten. You arrange your morning around it. You become surprised when nobody appears. You begin looking for an explanation.

      The last is the kind of case that interests us here.

      A conviction is something a person is convinced of. But that only moves the problem further, to a definition of being convinced.

      We can begin with a clear central case:

      A person who is convinced of something ordinarily relies on it as a basis for further expectation, interpretation, reasoning, judgment, or action without even getting the idea that there is something to settle at that moment.

      That means being convinced is not directly observable in another person. No single behavior reveals it. People can speak and act under instruction, coercion, convention, habit, strategy, or on the basis of assumptions they do not themselves believe. What we observe instead are patterns of reliance, expectation, surprise, reconsideration, and action from which conviction can be inferred.

      Convictions can shape interpretation and reasoning, guide judgment and action, and affect what appears surprising, threatening, relevant, possible, admirable, or absurd.

      But they do not need to be simply present or absent.

      Sometimes the language of degree seems natural.

      "I think Peter did it, but I'm not sure."

      "I'm almost certain the train leaves at six."

      "I have a vague suspicion that something is wrong."

      In such cases, how confident we are and whether we rely on something can come apart. We may rely on something while remaining uncertain about it, or be highly confident about something that matters little for what we do next.

      Conviction also need not be consistent throughout a person. Different parts of their orientation can support incompatible patterns of reliance at the same time.

      Still, these cases resemble the clear case closely enough that it remains useful to call them convictions.

      The word belief will remain useful in its ordinary broad sense. Conviction will be used when the convincing force of something, or the way it participates in further orientation, is specifically at issue.

      Convictions need not be explicitly formulated.

      You may never have stated that a certain friend is dependable. Yet you call them first when something goes wrong.

      A skilled programmer may rely on distinctions that would take considerable effort to put into words. Someone walking through a familiar city may expect a street to continue behind a building without ever explicitly thinking, “The street continues behind that building.”

      Much of what guides us operates without first becoming a sentence.

      This also limits how directly we can inspect it. We sometimes discover what mattered to us only after acting, hesitating, regretting, or being challenged.

      We do not have complete access to conviction formation, but we seem to be able to observe enough of it to investigate it.
