      • 11. What Conviction Formation Gives Us

      4 minStefan Kober

      We began with a practical problem.

      What can finite agents investigate and do while larger disputes about truth, justification, rationality, and foundations remain unresolved?

      The preceding investigation gives us a partial answer.

      Questions concerning how convictions form, stabilize, weaken, and change can be investigated without first settling those larger disputes.

      This does not make truth or justification irrelevant.

      But inquiry into conviction formation can begin before they are finally settled.

      The same applies to Conviction Formation Theory itself.

      CFT need not first establish itself as the final truth about conviction formation before it can be examined, used, challenged, or revised.

      Being convincing does not make it true.

      Its claims can still turn out to be untenable.

      Participation Without Control

      The fundamental claim was initially negative:

      Becoming convinced is not under direct voluntary control.

      But lack of direct control does not leave us passive.

      We can ask questions, seek information, introduce alternatives, compare, practise, expose assumptions, and invite criticism. We can make criteria explicit, defend convictions, and examine defenses.

      These interventions can change the conditions under which conviction forms.

      They do not determine the result.

      Participation does not require a standpoint outside conviction formation.

      The same limit applies when inquiry is shared.

      People can agree on procedures, use shared criteria, compare alternatives, and require claims to be defended.

      They can organize inquiry together.

      They cannot simply choose what its participants will find convincing. What becomes convincing among them must itself emerge and stabilize through the inquiry.

      Settlement Without Final Settlement

      Inquiry would be impossible if everything had to remain open at once.

      Convictions stabilize.

      Practices stabilize.

      Some parts of orientation become stable enough to function as criteria.

      Some criteria become shared strongly enough to organize inquiry across people.

      Such stability need not be permanent or universal.

      It can be local to a person, a group, a practice, a set of conditions, or a period of time.

      This gives us usable settlement. What has settled can carry further inquiry until something disturbs it.

      That disturbance may come from a contradiction, a failed expectation, a question, another person, a competing criterion, or something else that acquires enough force.

      Stability matters without vindicating what has stabilized.

      A stable conviction can be mistaken. A shared criterion can later be challenged, and settled practices can change.

      CFT treats settlement as something that happens within conviction formation, not as an endpoint outside it.

      Local settlement can still do some of the work for which we might otherwise demand a firm scholarly foundation first. It can support further inquiry, coordinate action, and allow reasoning to continue while larger questions remain open.

      Criteria and Shared Inquiry

      Criteria do not have to be supplied from somewhere beyond conviction formation.

      A distinction, aim, procedure, or standard can become convincing and stable enough to organize later assessment.

      Criteria can be proposed, compared, and applied without putting their convincing force under our control.

      Once operative, however, criteria can genuinely constrain an inquiry.

      Where enough criteria are shared, people can assess something together without reopening every standard at every step.

      Where they are not shared, disagreement may move to the criteria themselves.

      Challenge introduces another important possibility.

      A conviction that once operated within one person's orientation can become answerable to another.

      Someone asks why. A defense is offered.

      What is offered can be examined under criteria available in the inquiry.

      Conviction has become available for assessment across perspectives.

      Locating Disagreement

      People may disagree about what happened, which possibilities deserve attention, which considerations matter, or which criteria to use. They may even share a criterion but disagree about how it applies, or agree about the assessment without finding the same conclusion convincing. CFT does not settle these disagreements merely by distinguishing them.

      But sometimes progress consists in discovering where the disagreement actually lies.

      A question may become more precise, another possibility may need attention, or the inquiry may shift to a criterion or comparison.

      Inquiry Without Command

      Conviction Formation Theory does not give us a final theory of truth, a universal standard of justification, criteria everyone must accept, or a standpoint outside conviction formation from which everything can finally be judged.

      It gives us something more limited.

      Convictions form against an orientation already in place, and they can stabilize or become unsettled again. Questions can disturb what had seemed settled. Criteria can become stable enough to guide inquiry and shared enough to support assessment across people.

      Throughout this, finite agents can intervene without directly choosing what will convince them or anyone else.

      We can alter conditions, ask questions, compare possibilities, expose assumptions, apply criteria, challenge and defend.

      We can work with what has become convincing without pretending that it is final.
