      • Questions Between People

      2 minStefan Kober

      Questions also pass between people.

      If I ask: "Do you know where the key is?"

      I do more than register a gap in my own orientation.

      I treat you as someone who may be able to fill it.

      Other questions do more.

      A teacher may ask a student to identify an assumption. A doctor might want to know which symptoms appeared first, while an investigator asks a witness what happened before the alarm. An engineer might ask whether anyone checked name resolution before replacing the server.

      A friend asks: "Are you sure that is why she did it?"

      A question can introduce a distinction or possibility that was not previously active for the person being asked.

      It can make an assumption visible, demand a defense, redirect attention, or narrow the available responses.

      Questions between people are not automatically neutral.

      A lawyer may ask a question designed to restrict what can be said, while an interrogator may use one strategically. A politician might pose a question to close inquiry rather than open it. Teachers sometimes ask questions whose answers they already know, and a friend may ask simply because something in another person's account seems incomplete.

      Inquiry can be shared without being symmetrical.

      Who asks, who answers, and what relation exists between them affects what the question does.

      We can deliberately introduce questions not only into our own inquiry, but into the conditions under which conviction forms for other people.

      We cannot decide that the question will become puzzling for them.

      It may leave their orientation untouched. They may have an answer ready, dismiss the question, misunderstand it, or simply not find it important.

      But they may also discover, in trying to answer, that something they had taken for granted is harder to account for than they expected.

      The question can then acquire a force that neither the person asking it nor the person answering it directly chose. Questioning another person is another form of indirect participation in conviction formation.
